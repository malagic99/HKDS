Doctrine

Layered detection. Distributed fire. Defensive by design.

HKDS is a fixed terminal-defense layer. Its geometry, sensor architecture and engagement doctrine all enforce a defensive role — and turn the attacker's economic calculus on its head.

Sensor perimeter

A 10 km layered sensor mesh — not a wall

The perimeter transforms HKDS from a reactive point defense into a proactive kill-solution system. Detection at 10 km provides time to build a high-confidence firing solution before any target reaches engagement range. Multiple overlapping passive and active modalities ensure no threat profile passes undetected.

Zone functions

Detection → Preparation → Kill

  • Detection ring · 10 km

    All sensors active. Target detected, classified, handed to FLP. Trajectory model and maneuver envelope begin immediately.

  • Preparation ring · 5 km

    Firing solution approaches confidence threshold. Turrets pre-slew, belt rounds index, classification and weapon assignment lock.

  • Kill ring · 2 km

    Fire authority active. At 5 km/s muzzle velocity, a threat at 2 km is struck in under 0.4 s of flight.

FBG sensor data feeds directly into the same PRC layer that drives fire control. No protocol translation, no analogue-to-digital conversion step. Detection, tracking and fire control are a single continuous photonic computation from the moment a target enters sensor range.

Distributed battery

Corner nodes, cross-engagement, graceful degradation

Turret nodes deploy at the corners of the defended perimeter, not in a centralized cluster. Spatial distribution provides overlapping fire coverage, cross-engagement geometry, parallax-enhanced tracking and resilience against targeted suppression.

Cross-engagement doctrine

Each node defends its neighbour

When an adversary fires suppression rounds at the corner nodes, HKDS assigns each node to engage the threat targeting its neighbour, not the one targeting itself. NW engages threats inbound on NE; NE engages threats inbound on NW.

This is a geometric problem for the attacker: any suppression round must first survive cross-fire from another node before it can destroy its target. The attacker cannot suppress the defense without first defeating it.

Graceful degradation

Designed to degrade, not collapse

  • 1 of 4 lost — 75 % capacity

    All approach vectors retain ≥ 1 favorable engagement angle. Cross-engagement partially preserved.

  • 2 of 4 lost — 50 % capacity

    Coverage gaps appear on specific vectors. Functional defense against primary threat axes maintained.

  • FLP center lost

    Corner nodes fall back to autonomous operation via local FLP sub-nodes. Reduced coordination quality, continued engagement capability.

Helium infrastructure. A networked installation justifies shared helium infrastructure: central underground storage, buried supply lines pre-warmed against Joule-Thomson cooling, and a closed-loop recovery system targeting 70–80 % per shot. Beyond economics, recovery is a strategic supply-chain resilience measure — helium reserves are geopolitically concentrated and finite.

Hypersonic engagement

Competitive velocity — not chasing

HKDS does not claim to perfectly solve hypersonic defense. It proposes a kinetic terminal defense architecture more physically plausible than any currently deployed system. HKDS fires into an HGV's path — fundamentally different from missile interceptors that must chase.

Atmospheric drag

FLP solves the trajectory, not a correction

Below ~20 km, aerodynamic drag is significant for a 5 km/s round. FLP computes the ballistic trajectory with the full atmospheric model included — not as a perturbation. Engaging HGVs at high elevation (above 45°) routes the round through thinner atmosphere; the star turret's 85° elevation range accommodates this.

Plasma sheath

Optical sensing through RF blackout

Hypersonic threats generate plasma sheaths that degrade RF tracking during the terminal phase — when conventional fire control most needs it. FBG sensors and the photonic processing chain are optical, immune to RF plasma effects. An unplanned but significant advantage at the highest threat tier.

Doctrine

Defensive only, by architecture and by intent

HKDS is explicitly designed as a defensive, terminal area-denial system. Its geometry, emplacement and engagement logic all enforce a defensive role rather than offensive power projection.

What HKDS is

A fixed, autonomous, multi-threat terminal defense layer that engages incoming aerial objects only after they enter a defined defended airspace. An anti-access / area-denial shield protecting bases, cities and critical infrastructure from above.

  • Fixed-site architecture

    Corner turrets and buried sensor rings tie HKDS to the asset it protects; it is not a mobile artillery piece.

  • Incoming-only engagement

    Rules of engagement restrict HKDS to positively identified, inbound aerial threats within the defended volume.

  • Purely kinetic payloads

    No explosive warheads, no guidance seekers — just tungsten rods driven to hypervelocity.

  • Phalanx-precedent autonomy

    Campaign-level authorization with engagement-level autonomy follows the precedent of existing close-in weapon systems.
